Abstract

Driving-scene generators each consume their own conditioning: an image generator a rasterised bird's-eye-view map with projected boxes, a video generator a multi-view clip with a rendered HD map, an occupancy generator a voxelised layout. The scene a practitioner already has seldom arrives in any of these forms. We define UniSIR, a unified scene intermediate representation, translate inputs of different types — annotations, simulator files, or a sentence — and compile it into a backend's native conditioning through a small adapter. Because the representation is plugged into the interfaces the backends were trained on rather than replacing them, the question we can answer is whether the adapter costs anything, and we answer it by measurement rather than assertion: across the image, video and occupancy generators, the compiled conditioning is not worse than each model's own.

Method

Representation

A document is one frame in a single ego frame (x right, y forward, z up, metres): the ego state and its intended path, actors as oriented boxes with velocity, the map as polylines with a semantic kind, camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and the environment. Every block is paired with a modality mask that records what the input actually supplied, so a document states what it can and cannot fill before anything is compiled: the same type describes a nuScenes annotation, a simulator frame, and a document made from a sentence.

Adaptation

An adapter is a pure function from a document to a backend's payload, with no training and no learned parameters. The map is rasterised into eight binary planes at 0.5 m over ±50 m for the image backend; the same document is projected into a six-view clip with a rendered HD-map canvas for the video backend; and it is voxelised into an 18×200×200 layout over ±40 m for the occupancy backend, whose native slot is that tensor, so there the representation is the input rather than a translation of it. The three implementations are representatives of their families, chosen for convenience rather than scope.

Verification

Before anything is rendered, every payload is checked against the backend's own capability: shape and range, camera conventions against the backend's loader, and temporal consistency on the rendered frames. A payload either compiles whole or is rejected with the reason, which is what lets us treat the numbers below as a property of the representation rather than of a lucky run.

Results

1. Long-tail scenarios, rendered end to end

Each clip is 20–25 frames at 4 fps across six views, rendered by the video backend from a single document. None of the scenes exists in the dataset: they are constructed, simulated, checked for clearance, and then rendered.

2. The occupancy backend

UniScene's native slot is the layout tensor itself, so the document is compiled straight into it.

3. Qualitative results

The representation can also be used to construct scenes rather than translate them: a conflict is specified, simulated, written as a document, and rendered by whichever backend is available.
